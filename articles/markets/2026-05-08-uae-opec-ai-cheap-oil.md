# Maybe the Real Bull Market Hasn't Even Started

**Date:** 2026-05-08

---

Maybe the real bull market hasn't even started yet

If you see the 1973 oil crisis as "the end of the cheap-oil era,"
then the UAE leaving OPEC+ may be the signal that the global oil order is starting to loosen:
"the end of the expensive-oil era"

However turbulent the world economy was during the oil crises of the past,
that is how prosperous it could become in a long-term downcycle in oil prices

When a leap in technology meets cheaper energy, each amplifies the other
The AI leg alone has already filled the future with imagination
Add a long-term decline in oil prices, and the bull market's two legs will carry it far, long and steady

The weight of this news isn't in how much extra capacity the UAE can bring online right away
In the short term, prices are still set by the blockade of the Strait of Hormuz
What the UAE is declaring by leaving OPEC+ is this: future oil pricing may no longer be dictated by a single alliance, but jointly determined by each country's public finances, production capacity, investment returns and geopolitics

The horror of 1973 wasn't just that oil got expensive. It was that it shattered three pillars of the postwar economy all at once: cheap energy, fixed exchange rates, and welfare expansion built on the illusion of high growth. Once oil went up, inflation, unemployment, fiscal deficits and the exchange-rate system all blew up together

So what's really interesting today isn't "will oil prices fall?"
It's this: if oil really does go lower, will it rewrite the entire economic narrative the way 1973 did?

My answer is yes, and the impact may run deeper than many people think.

The UAE's exit from OPEC is precisely the prelude to this new narrative. It hints that the constraints on oil supply are loosening, and that future oil prices may no longer be set only by output cuts, quotas and geopolitical risk, but could instead return to a pricing logic driven more by supply efficiency and market competition.

The previous bull-market narrative stood on only one leg:

the tech-leap cycle brought on by AI
Goldman Sachs and other market research have pointed out that AI could push down the cost curve by improving logistics, resource allocation, predictive maintenance and supply-chain efficiency, and might even lower the marginal incentive price for some oil and gas production. In other words, AI isn't only creating new demand; it is also reshaping the cost structure on the supply side
AI doesn't just lift GDP on its own. It pushes every industry's production function up a notch

Now the second leg has arrived: the productivity cycle driven by falling medium-term oil costs
People have always read lower oil prices as consumers saving a bit of money. The more important part is that they directly cut marginal costs for energy-hungry industries such as transport, logistics, manufacturing, aviation and chemicals. This isn't just fatter corporate margins; it's an efficiency gain across the whole supply chain

When a leap in technology meets cheaper energy, each amplifies the other
The AI leg alone has already filled the future with imagination.
Add a long-term decline in oil prices, and the bull market's two legs will carry it far, long and steady

So what really matters about the UAE's exit isn't whether it can move oil prices right away. It's that it makes the market believe the institutional constraints on oil supply are weakening, while AI and efficiency gains are turning low oil prices into a more sustainable macro narrative

So what's truly worth betting on this round isn't "oil will definitely fall." It's this:
if 1973 stood for rising energy costs destroying the old order, then low oil prices in the AI era may stand for falling energy costs repairing that order, and even driving a new wave of productivity expansion

This is how I'd define this era:
not simply inflation coming down, and not simply an AI bull market,
but a triple resonance of cheaper energy, smarter technology and more efficient capital
