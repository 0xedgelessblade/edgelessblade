# The 30-Year Yield at 5.6%: What's Left in the Treasury's Toolbox

**Date:** 2026-10-05

---

The 30-year US Treasury yield keeps climbing, at one point surging to about 5.6%, the highest since 2002.

Treasury Secretary Bessent stepped up buybacks starting in August. It only held for a moment, and yields kept rising.

Let's look at what tools the Treasury has left.

The Treasury isn't the Fed. It can't print money, and it can't set interest rates directly.
What Bessent can work with is "how America borrows, and how it buys back old debt."

• Buybacks of old long bonds: raised from 2 billion per operation to as much as 6 billion, running through November 4.
• Leverage rules for the big banks have been eased, giving them more room to take on Treasuries

Cards in the toolbox not yet played:
• The TGA, the Treasury's checking account, holds about 900 billion, a good chunk more than usual. The Treasury has said it could be used to buy back debt, but that hasn't formally kicked off yet
• It can issue fewer 30-year bonds and more short-term bills instead. The next issuance plan comes out November 4

With less supply at the long end, the term premium has a chance to ease a bit

The cost is just as direct: the average maturity of the debt gets shorter, interest costs track the Fed more closely, and refinancing becomes a hurdle to clear every quarter

Once short-term debt piles up, will rolling it over next year get even more crowded? So this is swapping risk, not eliminating it

And default risk always comes from short-term debt,
not long-term debt
